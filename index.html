Spot the Mistake

Each worked example contains one error. Can you find it?

Example 1

A student measures a wooden block: Length = 5 cm, Width = 3 cm. They calculate the volume.
V = l × w
V = 5 × 3
V = 15 cm³

Missing a dimension!

Volume needs three measurements: length, width, and height. This student only multiplied two numbers, which gives area (cm²), not volume (cm³). They forgot to measure the height of the block.

Example 2

A student measures a box: Length = 8 cm, Width = 4 cm, Height = 3 cm.
V = l + w + h
V = 8 + 4 + 3
V = 15 cm³

Wrong operation!

The student added the measurements instead of multiplying them. The correct formula is V = l × w × h. The answer should be 8 × 4 × 3 = 96 cm³.

Example 3

A student measures a pencil case: Length = 0.2 m, Width = 0.08 m, Height = 0.05 m.
V = l × w × h
V = 0.2 × 0.08 × 0.05
V = 0.0008 m³

Wrong units for the task!

The maths is technically correct, but measuring a small object in metres cubed (m³) gives a tiny, hard-to-read number. For classroom objects, we should measure in centimetres to get the answer in cm³. That would be 20 × 8 × 5 = 800 cm³.

Example 4

A student measures a cube: Length = 6 cm, Width = 6 cm, Height = 6 cm.
V = l × w × h
V = 6 × 6 × 6
V = 216 cm²

Wrong unit label!

The calculation (216) is correct, but the student wrote cm² (centimetres squared) instead of cm³ (centimetres cubed). cm² is for area. Since volume involves three dimensions being multiplied, the unit must be cm³.

Same Volume, Different Look

Does the shape of the container change the amount of liquid?

Why are measuring cylinders more accurate? A measuring cylinder is tall and narrow. This means even a tiny difference in volume shows up as a noticeable change in height, making it easier to read precisely. A beaker is short and wide, so the same volume change barely moves the water level.

Spot the Mistake

Each worked example contains one error. Can you find it?

Example 1

A student needs to find the weight of water. They pour the water into a measuring cylinder, place it on the electronic balance, and read the display.
Balance reads: 145 g

"The water weighs 145 g."

Forgot to subtract the container!

The 145 g includes the weight of the measuring cylinder and the water combined. The student needed to either use the tare button to zero the cylinder first, or weigh the empty cylinder separately and subtract it. Without doing this, their answer is too high.

Example 2

A student wants to weigh some sand. They place a beaker on the balance and press Tare. The display shows 0.0 g. They then remove the beaker, pour sand directly onto the balance pan, and read the display.
After tare: 0.0 g
Removed beaker, added sand
Balance reads: 48.2 g

"The sand weighs 48.2 g."

Removed the container after pressing tare!

The student tared with the beaker on the balance, then took the beaker off. This means the balance is now accounting for a beaker that is not there. The reading will be incorrect. They should have left the beaker on the balance and added the sand into the beaker.

Example 3

A student is finding the weight of air. They weigh an empty balloon and a balloon filled with air.
Empty balloon: 2.1 g
Balloon with air: 2.1 g

"Air has no mass because the weight didn't change."

Wrong conclusion!

Air does have mass, but it is very light. The difference might be too small for this balance to detect (e.g. 0.01 g). A more sensitive balance, or a larger balloon, would show a small increase. The correct conclusion is that air has mass, but it is very small compared to solids and liquids.

Example 4

A student weighs an empty measuring cylinder, then weighs it again with water inside. They calculate the weight of the water.
Empty cylinder: 85 g
Cylinder + water: 120 g

Water weight = 120 + 85 = 205 g

Added instead of subtracting!

To find the weight of just the water, you need to subtract the container's weight from the total. The correct calculation is: 120 − 85 = 35 g. Adding gives a number larger than the total, which should be a clue that something is wrong.

Practical Activity: Weight

Part A: Subtraction Method

Find 3 things in the room. Weigh the empty container, then weigh again with contents inside, and subtract.

1
Weigh the empty container. Record.
2
Add your contents. Record the new total.
3
Subtract to find the weight of just the contents.
Contents = Total − Container

Part B: Tare Method

Find 3 different things in the room. Use the tare button so the balance only shows the weight of what you add.

1
Place the empty container on the balance.
2
Press TARE. Display reads 0.0 g.
3
Add contents into the container. Record the weight shown.
Do not remove the container after pressing Tare!
Remember: Record all weights in grams (g). Make sure the balance reads 0.0 g before you start. Record your results in the worksheet provided.
